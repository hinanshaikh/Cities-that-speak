Why sound?

Some sounds are so tied to a place that hearing them takes me straight back there. I have always recorded my travels with photographs and videos, but they never kept what a city sounds like. The azaan echoing across rooftops in Lahore, the chime of the London Underground doors: these hold a meaning a photograph can't.

So this project asks one question: what is it about sound that builds belonging? There is no narration. The sounds tell the story, and the moment between the two cities is the centre of the piece. London fades back towards that crossing at the end, so neither city has the final word.

Lahore

The city I grew up in

For me, Lahore sounds like familiarity and childhood: the loud chatter of people, busy streets, and the azaan rising over everything five times a day. In the soundscape, Lahore's sounds sit close together and overlap. The azaan, the vendors' calls and the traffic are stacked on top of each other, because that is how the city feels to me day to day: warm, spontaneous and close.

  • Azaan, the call to prayer at dawn
  • Street vendors calling out their goods
  • Rickshaws and motorbikes in the traffic
  • Dhol, the drum of Punjabi celebrations

Recorded at Liberty Market and in the inner city, September 2026.

London

A second home, discovered over many visits

London's crowded streets, full of street performers, musicians, transport announcements and overlapping conversations, made me curious about the world outside. In the soundscape, London's sounds are given more space. The Tube chime and the station announcement stand apart from the sirens and the street chatter, reflecting the more structured rhythm I feel there.

  • Tube chimes and station announcements
  • Street performers and buskers
  • Pedestrian crossings and traffic signals
  • Sirens moving through the streets

How I made it

What I set out to learn

I wanted to understand how the everyday sounds of a city create a sense of place and identity, and to learn how to record, edit and compose 3D sound so a listener who has never been to Lahore or London can feel the contrast between them, and what it is like to be in between.

  • RecordingEvery sound was recorded by me in the field, in stereo, on a Zoom recorder with a windshield. A test in Week 2 showed that mono sounded flat, "inside my head", so I never used it.
  • Choosing soundsI used R. Murray Schafer's idea of keynote sounds and checked each one against research. The dhol was added after research. Big Ben was removed because it is mostly heard by tourists, not in everyday London life.
  • LayeringEach part has at least three layers: a background hum, a middle layer like traffic or footsteps, and a foreground voice or call.
  • Moving soundI panned sounds around the listener in Audacity and GarageBand, and used volume to make them feel near or far.
  • RespectPrivate conversations are blurred, and the azaan is never mixed with unsuitable sound.
